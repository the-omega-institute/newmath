\subsection{Cross-organism CUN/UUR Leu gate}\label{subsec:h3-translation-realization-cross-organism-cun-uur-leu-gate}
\origin{ai}

\paragraph{Finite packet.}
This record treats the leucine codon split between the $CUN$ family and the $UUR$ family as an organism-level statistical contact, not as a direct statement about protein output.  The carrier is the residual coordinate obtained from the sign and magnitude of $\log(CUN/UUR)$ after keeping the organism label and an amino-acid-composition control in view.  The paired readout is the organism-level spectrum of tRNA-Leu gene-copy counts by anticodon family.  The finite organism row used here contains exactly three loaded organisms: \texttt{homo\_sapiens}, \texttt{saccharomyces\_cerevisiae}, and \texttt{escherichia\_coli\_k12}.  The test asks whether the $CUN/UUR$ sign is non-uniform across those organisms and whether its magnitude is positively associated with the corresponding tRNA-Leu copy-number ratio after the stated composition control.

\paragraph{Internal codon coordinate.}
The supporting code-table contact also records the finite set $M=WNR\cup CUN$ with cardinality $20$.  In the recorded order this set is
$$
\begin{aligned}
M=\{&AAA,AAG,ACA,ACG,AGA,AGG,AUA,AUG,CUA,CUC,\\
& CUG,CUU,UAA,UAG,UCA,UCG,UGA,UGG,UUA,UUG\}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This list is used only to name the codon-window coordinate in which the $CUN$ branch is being inspected.  It does not supply organism-specific usage by itself.  The biological contact comes from per-organism codon-usage summaries paired with organism-level tRNA-Leu gene-copy spectra; without that pairing, the codon labels alone would remain a code-read coordinate rather than evidence for a translation-layer relation.

\paragraph{Measured row.}
The finite validation row reports non-uniform signs for the three organisms.  The recorded logarithmic ratios are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\log(CUN/UUR)&=(1.3514980212896148,\ -0.24869078488829677,\ 1.0086640520051702),\\
\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\log(CUN/UUR)\bigr)&=(1,\ -1,\ 1).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the row explicitly rejects uniform sign behavior: \texttt{sign\_uniform=false}.  The same validation run reports a Pearson value of $1.0$ for the paired statistic and a positive partial correlation, after amino-acid-composition control, with $n=3$ and partial Pearson value $1.0$.  The experiment identifier is \texttt{cross\_organism\_cun\_uur\_leu\_gate\_validation}, and the recorded status is \texttt{passed}.  The finite conclusion is therefore narrow: in this three-organism row, the $CUN/UUR$ leucine usage coordinate changes sign across organisms and the controlled paired statistic is positive against the tRNA-Leu copy-number ratio.

\paragraph{Scientific reading.}
The row supports a bounded cross-organism translation-realization contact only in the statistical sense just stated.  Human, yeast, and \textit{E. coli} K-12 do not share a single observed sign for $\log(CUN/UUR)$, and the available paired statistic aligns the sign-and-magnitude coordinate with the tRNA-Leu gene-copy coordinate under the listed control.  This is enough to keep the packet as a concrete organism-level usage/copy-number relation.  It is not enough to infer a universal direction for leucine codon choice, because one of the three signs is negative while two are positive, and the sample size is only $3$.

\paragraph{Local exclusion boundary.}
No full translation process, ribosome-mediated mechanism, folded structure, physical admissibility, biological function, or universal biological law follows from this row alone.  The codon-usage table contact summarizes coding-sequence composition and sampling choices; the tRNA contact summarizes genome-annotation copy numbers.  Neither contact observes ribosome dynamics, protein conformation, cellular function, or admissibility in a physical model.  Any such stronger statement would require a separate contact with the relevant measured object.  In particular, raw $\log(CUN/UUR)$ without amino-acid-composition control is not accepted here as a translation claim, and the observed sign reversal is reported as context-dependent rather than as a general rule.
