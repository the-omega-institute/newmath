\subsection{mTOR-linked translation ranking as a bounded cross-layer readout}
\label{sec:bioreality-mtor-translation-topscore-hsieh2012}

\paragraph{Finite row.}
The Hsieh-2012 mTOR translation packet is retained as a finite cross-layer row, not as a mechanistic reconstruction of mTOR control.  Its executable contact record has run identifier \texttt{4b61f2a99385}, joins $n=1818$ rows, and finishes in $3.328$ seconds.  This is enough to certify that a same-row statistical comparison was carried out for the named mTOR-translation readout, but it is deliberately too small to infer a pathway, a regulatory model, or a universal translation principle.  The row therefore enters the BioReality ledger as a bounded score-bearing observation: it can be compared with other translation and protein-abundance readouts, but it cannot by itself identify the biochemical source of the signal.

\paragraph{Scoring rule.}
The local ranking language used around this row separates absorption from direction.  For a candidate readout $H$, the reported quantities are the absorbed energy $A_H$, the coordinate-support cosine $C_H$, and the penalized score
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Score}(H) &= A_H C_H - \lambda\,\mathrm{DL}(H),\\
\lambda &= 0.01,\\
\mathrm{DL}(H) &= \log_2(1+n_{\mathrm{columns}}).
\end{aligned}
$$
For one-column readouts this gives $\mathrm{DL}=1$, while the twelve-column localization readout has $\mathrm{DL}=3.700439718141092$.  The rule is useful because it prevents a high-energy but misdirected or overly broad readout from being promoted.  For example, in the human ranking table, PTM density has the largest raw absorption among one-column protein features, $A_H=0.014884825920744861$, but only $C_H=0.3604440741749213$, giving $A_HC_H=0.0053651472982577525$ and $\mathrm{Score}=-0.004634852701742248$.  Human complex membership has less absorption, $A_H=0.007527199987780841$, but stronger directional agreement, $C_H=0.8723071163246565$, giving $A_HC_H=0.006566030115340095$ and the top human one-column entry, still with negative penalized score $-0.0034339698846599055$.

\paragraph{Cross-organism comparison.}
The finite ranking table shows that no single readout is shared as a universal absorber.  The best Danio rerio entry is measured translation efficiency with $A_H=0.017594290015070787$, $C_H=0.6573753096184687$, $A_HC_H=0.011566051846174291$, and $\mathrm{Score}=0.001566051846174291$ over $n=13140$ joined rows.  Its mRNA row is close, with $A_HC_H=0.01073458857797759$ and $\mathrm{Score}=0.000734588577977589$.  Human instead ranks complex membership first, with mRNA next at $A_H=0.007858222847072393$, $C_H=0.7921354915786852$, $A_HC_H=0.006224777217900546$, and $\mathrm{Score}=-0.0037752227820994546$.  Saccharomyces cerevisiae ranks mRNA first, but the signal is much smaller: $A_H=0.001897605472160837$, $C_H=0.6040571890503664$, $A_HC_H=0.0011462622274400686$, and $\mathrm{Score}=-0.008853737772559931$.  Escherichia coli K-12 has only mRNA and measured-TE rows in this panel, both below the Bonferroni gate; mRNA gives $A_HC_H=0.0015297976209788734$ and $\mathrm{Score}=-0.008470202379021127$.  Gallus gallus has a top complex-membership row with $A_H=0.0074424743012025205$, $C_H=0.5699065404576333$, $A_HC_H=0.004241514781443171$, and $\mathrm{Score}=-0.00575848521855683$.  Thus the mTOR row is interpreted against a background in which top-scoring translation-adjacent and protein-feature absorbers are organism-specific and mostly weak after the fixed length penalty.

\paragraph{Translation context.}
The measured translation-efficiency decomposition gives a useful scale for the mTOR row.  The measured mediated fractions are heterogeneous: yeast has $M^{QTP}_{\mathrm{meas}}=0.4793069656345167$, E. coli has $0.04091158340950673$, human has $0.0027966485036786744$, and Danio has a raw suppressive value $-0.04801486048721136$ clipped to a reported mediated fraction of $0$.  The modeled-complement residuals are also organism-specific: $d=0.21173219572706703$ for yeast, $0.6629960038578532$ for E. coli, $0.874628721049266$ for Danio, $0.9208949355679455$ for human, $0.9400341806302775$ for Gallus, and $0.8972672018746489$ for Mycobacterium smegmatis.  The measured tRNA-copy tAI absorber does not remove the residual either: Danio has $A_H=0.020817102845637092$, human has $5.426229300865148\times 10^{-5}$, and yeast has $0.001530747712165342$.  These values keep the Hsieh-2012 mTOR entry in a descriptive position: it may sit among translation-associated readouts, but the surrounding finite rows do not license a claim that mTOR is the missing universal channel for the residual.

\paragraph{Boundary signals and negative evidence.}
The broader coordinate ledger contains strong finite facts that still do not upgrade the mTOR row into a mechanism.  The Window6 boundary $R$ set is avoided in all $15$ computed organisms, with $15$ avoided, $0$ preferred, and a one-sided binomial value $p=3.0517578125\times 10^{-5}$; among significant organisms, the avoided direction appears in $6/6$ cases.  The optimal-codon direction survey is classified as \texttt{clustered}, with mean pairwise cosine $0.2629282074265475$ across $15$ computed organisms and $41$ synonymous design columns.  The organism-specificity meta-test, however, does not resolve a clean domain mechanism: the dominant-coordinate contingency has $n=12$, Cram\'er value $0.6258327785172862$, and right-tail permutation value $0.5814185814185814$.  The M-fraction associations with GC3, log proteome size, and log tRNA gene count are likewise non-decisive, with permutation values $0.35264735264735264$, $0.913086913086913$, and $0.8621378621378621$.  These numbers say that there is real finite structure in the coordinate readback, but not a settled biological module or universal regulatory explanation.

\paragraph{Data coverage.}
Coverage limits are part of the statement.  The yeast turnover contact joins $3640$ proteins, with reported CDS hit rate $0.9156038419146592$ and turnover join hit rate $0.8818572656921754$; its absorbed turnover fraction is only $0.0009086031889966327$.  The human turnover contact joins $8057$ proteins out of a full human complement row count of $19053$, with CDS hit rate $0.9779294769799313$ and absorbed fraction $3.5740764900116538\times 10^{-6}$.  The f3-stress reach calculation uses $512$ candidate subsets and finds best coverage $0.22618445168998694$ against a selection-aware null 95th percentile of $0.02697141300639046$, leaving $0.7738155483100131$ unexplained after the full readout set.  Ordered residual decompositions make the same point: unexplained fractions remain $0.9756319042010854$ for Danio, $0.9942823785118229$ for E. coli, $0.9798666209022441$ for human in the direction-aware table, and $0.8676586345294415$ for yeast.  The minimal-compilation check reports median compressibility $0.026$ and organism-level compressibilities $0.024368095798914635$, $0.005717621488177116$, $0.02635576736932188$, and $0.13234136547055853$ for Danio, E. coli, human, and yeast respectively.  Hence the mTOR-linked finite row is surrounded by a mostly unexplained residual field rather than by a compact shared explanation.

\paragraph{Local interpretation rule.}
The admissible conclusion is narrow.  The Hsieh-2012 mTOR translation entry contributes one computed cross-dataset row with $n=1818$ to a finite panel of translation-adjacent and protein-feature readouts.  It can be ranked, compared, and used to flag where additional contact rows would be valuable.  It does not establish translation realization beyond the measured contact, folding, physical admissibility, biological function, causal mediation, protein-state mechanism, a universal absorber $H$, or a global biological law.  Any such stronger statement would require a separate same-layer contact with its own statistic, null model, and row-level provenance.
