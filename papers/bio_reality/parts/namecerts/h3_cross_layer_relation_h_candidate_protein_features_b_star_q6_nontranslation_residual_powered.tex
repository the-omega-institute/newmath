\subsection{Protein-feature absorption of the $B^*_{Q6}$ modeled-translation complement}
\label{sec:q6-protein-feature-absorption-boundary}

\paragraph{Finite surface.}
The record tests whether coarse protein-feature annotations absorb any part of the $B^*_{Q6}$ residual component left after the modeled-translation projection has already been removed.  The carrier is a finite CDS-by-protein surface indexed by three organisms, nine residual coordinates, and four one-dimensional feature candidates.  The residual coordinates are
$K\_AAA$, $Arg\_AGR$, $Ile\_AUA$, $Leu\_CUN\_vs\_UUR$, $Leu\_UUA\_vs\_UUG$, $Ser\_UCR\_vs\_AGY$, $Ser\_UCA\_vs\_UCG$, $Thr\_ACR\_vs\_ACY$, and $f3\_stress$.
For each organism, the control matrix has $24$ columns and rank $23$, with controls consisting of an intercept, $\log(\mathrm{CDS\ length\ in\ nt})$, the $20$ amino-acid composition proportions from real CDS codon counts, $GC3$, and an $M$-density baseline defined from the union support of the nine projected $B^*_{Q6}$ vectors.  The feature candidates are $complex\_member$, $domain\_count$, $ptm\_density$, and $tm\_count$, each with candidate rank $K_c=1$ on each available organism.

\paragraph{Join geometry.}
The feature surface is not the full abundance-CDS surface.  The zebrafish row joins $2242$ proteins with feature data out of $3180$ feature records and $14410$ reported CDS-abundance joins.  The human row joins $18335$ proteins out of $18842$ feature records and $19053$ reported CDS-abundance joins.  The yeast row joins $5682$ proteins out of $6156$ feature records and $5815$ reported CDS-abundance joins.  Thus the finite table is large enough to compute bounded projections, but it is not invariant under changing the readout requirement.

\paragraph{Modeled-complement stability check.}
Let $d_{model}$ denote the fraction of the $B^*_{Q6}$ abundance component left after the modeled-translation component is removed on the row set being analyzed.  The feature-row values are compared against the full abundance-CDS complement reference:
$$
\begin{aligned}
 d_{model}^{Drerio,feature} &= 0.7361678489976374, & d_{model}^{Drerio,full} &= 0.874628721049266, & |\Delta| &= 0.1384608720516286, \\
 d_{model}^{Human,feature} &= 0.9188293375787641, & d_{model}^{Human,full} &= 0.9208949355679455, & |\Delta| &= 0.002065597989181489, \\
 d_{model}^{Yeast,feature} &= 0.21235197749007645, & d_{model}^{Yeast,full} &= 0.21173219572706703, & |\Delta| &= 0.0006197817630094193.
\end{aligned}
$$
The human and yeast feature joins preserve the modeled-complement reference within $0.02$, while the zebrafish feature join does not.  The zebrafish discrepancy is not a small numerical fluctuation: it reflects that sparse reviewed-feature availability changes the protein set on which the residual target is defined.

\paragraph{Feature absorption values.}
For each candidate feature $H_c$, the statistic reported here is the descriptive absorbed fraction of the modeled-translation complement after residualizing both the complement and the candidate against the declared controls.  The observed absorptions are
$$
\begin{aligned}
Drerio:&& complex &= 0.00039566384726157463, & domain &= 0.0008268196005307971, & ptm &= 0.014211240113472575, & tm &= 0.001369142724157357, \\
Human:&& complex &= 0.0075271999877804495, & domain &= 0.000011405527940317433, & ptm &= 0.014884825920540636, & tm &= 0.0015569064882873212, \\
Yeast:&& complex &= 0.00069212177545487, & domain &= 0.00007029698692511431, & ptm &= 0.0005759336988655581, & tm &= 0.00031741485098156394.
\end{aligned}
$$
The largest single descriptive value is $0.014884825920540636$ for human $ptm\_density$, closely followed by $0.014211240113472575$ for zebrafish $ptm\_density$.  These numbers are small in absolute scale, so the feature surface is an explanatory-boundary test rather than a strong reconstruction of the residual component.

\paragraph{Degree-of-freedom null comparison.}
The Bonferroni-screened one-degree-of-freedom null marks only a subset of these descriptive absorptions as excess over the matched null.  In zebrafish, only $ptm\_density$ exceeds its threshold: observed absorption $0.014211240113472575$ exceeds the Bonferroni threshold $0.003775603188484381$, with null mean $0.0004610486745564995$ and null $95$th percentile $0.001788151234180097$ over $200$ trials.  In human, $complex\_member$, $ptm\_density$, and $tm\_count$ exceed their thresholds: the thresholds are $0.0006605662314432479$, $0.00042094892238120357$, and $0.0002697448894057371$, respectively.  Human $domain\_count$ does not exceed its threshold $0.0005934431422432757$.  In yeast, none of the four candidates exceeds its Bonferroni threshold; for example $ptm\_density$ has observed absorption $0.0005759336988655581$ against threshold $0.001713645678402473$, null mean $0.00017657229078902367$, and null $95$th percentile $0.0007361908685600422$.

\paragraph{Feature sparsity and annotation scale.}
The finite distributions show why the result must stay scoped.  Zebrafish has $211$ nonzero joined $ptm\_density$ records among $2242$ joined rows, with mean $0.0012176949624096891$ and $95$th percentile $0.003048780487804878$.  Human has $9210$ nonzero joined $ptm\_density$ records among $18335$ rows, with mean $0.006473867757948575$ and $95$th percentile $0.024291497975708502$.  Yeast has $2282$ nonzero joined $ptm\_density$ records among $5682$ rows, with mean $0.002774535811043876$ and $95$th percentile $0.012152777777777778$.  The annotation density and the row-selection effect are therefore organism-dependent, not a single stable cross-organism feature law.

\paragraph{Relation to direct translation readouts.}
The measured translation-efficiency contact gives an independent scale check.  On the same $B^*_{Q6}$ coordinate family and the same $24$-column, rank-$23$ control shape, measured ribosome-profiling translation efficiency absorbs $0.01759429001506974$ in zebrafish, $0.0010982956489726373$ in $E.\ coli$, $0.0028947149257963897$ in human, and $0.0002762783754375593$ in yeast.  The measured tRNA-gene-copy tAI contact absorbs $0.020817102845637092$ in zebrafish, $0.00005426229300865148$ in human, and $0.001530747712165342$ in yeast.  These comparison contacts do not turn the protein-feature result into a translation mechanism; they show only that the protein-feature absorptions sit beside separate bounded readouts rather than replacing them.

\paragraph{Verdict.}
The finite computation supports a narrow statement: reviewed UniProt or STRING-derived protein-feature annotations can be projected against the modeled-translation complement of the $B^*_{Q6}$ residual, and a few organism-feature pairs exceed a one-degree-of-freedom Bonferroni null.  It does not support a stable cross-organism $H$ closure, because the zebrafish feature join changes the modeled-complement target by $0.1384608720516286$ relative to the full abundance-CDS reference, and yeast has no Bonferroni-exceeding candidate.  The correct reading is therefore bounded absorption on available feature rows, not causal explanation.

\paragraph{Local boundary.}
No measured translation claim, folding claim, physical-admissibility claim, molecular-function claim, biological mechanism, phenotype statement, or universal biological rule follows from this feature projection alone.  A separate layer-matched contact is required for each such assertion.  In particular, $ptm\_density$, $tm\_count$, $domain\_count$, and $complex\_member$ are sparse annotation readouts, not direct observations of ribosome dynamics, folding pathways, physical feasibility, molecular function, or organism-level law.
