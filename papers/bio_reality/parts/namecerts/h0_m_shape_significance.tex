\paragraph{Finite codon display.}
The window-six codon packet studies the standard genetic-code table only at the level where a codon is a six-bit coordinate and where the observed label is an amino-acid or stop label assigned by the curated table. The biological object under inspection is therefore the standard-code codon table as a finite labelled cube, not translation as a physical process. The carrier is the codon cube $Q_6$, and the visible distinctions are the six-bit codon display, a local tile face, a singleton label corner, and a three-point label class. The readback contact is the standard-code label table: it gives codon-to-label assignments and nothing more.

\paragraph{Boundary set.}
The finite row identifies the boundary set $R$ with cardinality $13$. The recorded codons are
$$\begin{aligned}
R=\{&\mathrm{AAA},\mathrm{AGA},\mathrm{AGG},\mathrm{AUA},\mathrm{CUA},\mathrm{CUC},\mathrm{CUG},\mathrm{CUU},\\
&\mathrm{UAA},\mathrm{UAG},\mathrm{UCA},\mathrm{UGA},\mathrm{UUA}\}.
\end{aligned}$$
This same set is recorded as a punctured-cube shape with $f$-vector
$$\begin{aligned}
(f_0,f_1,f_2)=(13,16,4).
\end{aligned}$$
Thus the row does not merely count codons. It specifies a local combinatorial form: thirteen vertices, sixteen one-dimensional adjacencies, and four two-dimensional faces in the selected codon-coordinate region. The verified row reports that $R$ matches the expected codon list exactly, so the boundary object is finite, explicit, and reproducible from the row data.

\paragraph{Observed shape significance.}
The matching experiment for the $M$-shape used $N=2000$ sampled alternatives with seed $42$. Among these, there was $1$ exact shape match, giving
$$\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{exact}}=0.0005.
\end{aligned}$$
There were $25$ samples with Jaccard similarity at least $0.9$, giving
$$\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{Jaccard}\geq 0.9}=0.0125.
\end{aligned}$$
The sampling ledger records $57377$ distinct-sample attempts and $55377$ duplicate retries. The high retry count is important for interpretation: the finite comparison space contains many repeated draws under the imposed constraints, so the reported significance should be read as a constrained finite-row statistic rather than as an asymptotic biological law. Still, within the recorded null experiment, the observed local shape is rare both for exact matching and for high-overlap matching.

\paragraph{Amino-acid-class preserving null.}
A stricter null model preserves amino-acid class while reshuffling the relevant finite structure. In this row the observed $M$ size is $20$. Across $N=2000$ samples with seed $42$, the exact-match count is $0$, so the recorded exact-shape probability is
$$\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{class,exact}}=0.0.
\end{aligned}$$
The lower-tail count at or below the observed $M$ size is $7$, giving
$$\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{class,lower\ tail}}=0.0035.
\end{aligned}$$
This supplies a second finite check: even after retaining amino-acid-class information, the observed local object remains uncommon under the recorded sampling rule. The result supports a code-layer shape claim, but it does not identify a biochemical mechanism that would force the shape.

\paragraph{Scientific verdict and scope.}
The finite rows support the following code-layer conclusion. Window-six coordinates expose a rare local boundary-tile shape in the standard-code codon table: $|R|=13$, the punctured-cube vector is $(13,16,4)$, the exact-shape sampling probability is $0.0005$, the high-Jaccard probability is $0.0125$, the amino-acid-class preserving lower-tail probability is $0.0035$, and the leave-one experiments preserve high recovery. This is enough to record a significant local codon-label pattern. It is not enough to promote the pattern to translation realization, folded structure, physical admissibility, biological function, mechanism, or universality. Those stronger claims would require independent contacts at those layers. The curated standard-code table observes codon labels; it does not observe ribosomal realization, molecular folding, energetic feasibility, organismal function, or a law governing all biological coding systems.
