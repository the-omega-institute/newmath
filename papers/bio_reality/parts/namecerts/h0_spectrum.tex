\paragraph{Finite coordinate object.}
The object studied here is the standard-code codon table viewed through a six-bit codon display. The ambient carrier is the codon cube $Q_6$, with each codon represented as a finite coordinate row rather than as a biochemical mechanism. The reality contact is only the curated codon-to-label table: it says which codons carry amino-acid or stop labels in the standard code. The mathematical question is therefore local and finite. It asks whether the rows singled out by the code-layer boundary form a recognizable tile inside $Q_6$, and whether the associated adjacency data has a stable spectrum. The distinguished boundary set is
$$
\begin{aligned}
R=\{&AAA, AGA, AGG, AUA, CUA, CUC, CUG, CUU,\\
&UAA, UAG, UCA, UGA, UUA\}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The verified cardinality row gives $|R|=13$. The same thirteen codons appear in the punctured-cube check, where the associated face-count vector is
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(R)=(13,16,4).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the finite object is not being inferred from a continuum model or from molecular dynamics. It is a thirteen-vertex tile, with sixteen one-dimensional adjacencies and four two-dimensional faces, read against a curated label table.

This gives a compact numerical signature for the local codon-label split, but it does not by itself identify a translation apparatus, a folded structure, a physical admissibility criterion, a function, a mechanism, or a universal biological law.

\paragraph{Shape significance.}
The row-level shape test compares the observed tile against random samples. With seed $42$ and $N=2000$ sampled trials, there was $1$ exact shape match. The resulting exact-shape probability is
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{exact}}=0.0005.
\end{aligned}
$$
The same run made $57377$ distinct-sample attempts and incurred $55377$ duplicate retries, so the finite sampling space was heavily constrained by repeated proposals. A high-Jaccard comparison at threshold $0.9$ produced $25$ matches, giving
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{J\ge 0.9}=0.0125.
\end{aligned}
$$
These values support the statement that the observed local shape is uncommon under the sampled coordinate-preserving null. They do not promote the tile to a global law of DNA. They say that, inside the six-bit display and under the tested null, the standard-code boundary pattern is not a typical random tile.

It remains, however, a statement about codon labels and finite coordinates, not about how a ribosome realizes translation or how an amino-acid chain folds.

\paragraph{Local verdict.}
The evidence supports a code-read claim. In the six-bit codon cube, the standard-code label table determines a thirteen-codon boundary tile with face vector $(13,16,4)$, spectral radius $0.6752479308830213$, companion value $\lambda_R=0.4758352843197002$, parameter $\mu=3.051487585298128$, and a cubic residual near $10^{-11}$. Randomized finite tests give $p_{\mathrm{exact}}=0.0005$, $p_{J\ge 0.9}=0.0125$, and an amino-acid-class preserving lower-tail value $0.0035$. Recovery checks over $23$ code tables and $63$ targets give hit rate $0.9841269841269841$ above baseline, while leave-one-codon recovery is $1.0$. The chapter therefore records a finite, reality-anchored code-layer pattern: window-six coordinates expose a local boundary tile in the curated standard-code table. No conclusion about actual translation, folded protein structure, physical admissibility, biological function, mechanistic necessity, or universality follows from these rows unless an independent higher-layer contact is supplied.
