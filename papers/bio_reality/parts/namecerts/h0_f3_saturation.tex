\paragraph{Finite codon packet.}
The row indexed by $h0.f3.saturation$ records a finite codon computation whose biological reading is deliberately narrow.  The biological object is open-reading-frame eligibility as tested by start and stop codon enumeration, but the packet itself does not contain a sequence interval, a transcript, a translation product, or a functional assay. \begin{aligned}

&UAA, UAG, UCA, UGA, UUA\},
\end{aligned}
$$
with no missing and no extra elements in that motif union.  The companion marker row records $|M|=20$ and
$$
\begin{aligned}

&CUG,CUU,UAA,UAG,UCA,UCG,UGA,UGG,UUA,UUG\}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the finite row is not a free biological assertion: it is a closed enumeration of a codon-level combinatorial carrier together with an explicit list of the symbols that are present.

It does not say that any nucleotide interval has been observed in a biological database, nor that a ribosome reads the interval, nor that a peptide exists.

\paragraph{Quotient geometry used only as finite context.}

\paragraph{Boundary interpretation for ORF eligibility.}
As an ORF-eligibility seed, the packet is admissible only as a proposal for what would need to be checked once an independent biological interval is supplied.  The probes attached to the row enforce exactly this limitation.  The first probe says that start/stop-window eligibility must remain at boundary status unless a curated sequence, transcript, or annotation contact supplies an observed interval.  The second probe rejects any record that claims ORF eligibility while no sequence database entry, transcript evidence, or curated interval annotation can test it.  Consequently, the finite codon computation may organize the question, but it may not answer the biological question by itself.
